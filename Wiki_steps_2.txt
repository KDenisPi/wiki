  First: the premise in that spec text no longer holds

  Those two paragraphs were written for the Wikipedia markdown dataset — raw article prose, where an LLM must read a chunk and invent Q&A pairs, and where hallucination is the central risk. What you actually built is a
  structured fact database. That inverts the trade-off the spec describes:

  - "Synthetic generation" was the realistic option and "template-based" the reliable but rigid one.
  - With a DB, templates are hallucination-free by construction — every answer is a query result, not a model's reading of prose. The rigidity concern (overfitting to phrasings) is real but solvable separately.

  So the shape I'd argue for is a split of duties: templates + SQL produce the facts and answers; an LLM paraphrases only the questions, never touching the answer. You get phrasing variety with zero hallucination
  surface. That's strictly better than generating pairs from prose, and it's only available to you because of the work already done.

  The decision that gates everything

  "What do we need" depends entirely on what the model is for, and there are three answers:

  (a) Fine-tune to memorize facts — the spec's own opening paragraph argues against this, and it was right. Millions of pairs, and the model still answers confidently-wrong a meaningful fraction of the time.

  (b) RAG — then you don't need training pairs at all. You need an eval set (a few thousand verified pairs) and a retrieval index. Note your DB is a better retrieval target than article chunks for these questions: "what  happened in 1875 in music" is an aggregation, and no amount of chunk retrieval aggregates reliably.

  (c) Text-to-SQL — my recommendation. Your questions are literally aggregate queries: filter by year/century, by area, by country, by occupation. The DB answers them exactly. So make the training target {question → SQL}
  rather than {question → fact}. Consequences:
  - Dataset size drops from millions to 5–20K pairs — a fine-tune that can actually succeed.
  - Every pair is auto-verifiable: execute the generated SQL, compare to the reference result. No manual spot-checking of 100K pairs.
  - New facts need no retraining. Re-run the dump pass, and the same model answers with fresh data.
  - Failure mode is a broken query (visible, catchable) rather than a plausible lie.

  A hybrid is natural later: (c) for the aggregate questions, (b) for open-ended "tell me about X" once you join to article text.

  What we'd need, concretely

  1. Question taxonomy → SQL templates, ~6–8 shapes: point lookup ("when was X born"), reverse lookup ("who was born in 1473"), filtered list ("German scientists in the 1400s"), period+area ("what happened in 1875 in
  music"), aggregate ("how many composers died in the 18th century"), relational ("who composed X"). queries.sql is already the skeleton.
  2. A notability signal — see below, this is the time-critical one.
  3. Occupation → area mapping (science / music / art / politics). Query 7 gives the few thousand occupations that actually occur; that's a one-time classification job, cheap with an LLM.
  4. An answer policy for the messy cases, all of which are already measured: 2.23M dates are ye Jump to bottom (ctrl+End) ↓  be rendered as exact days; 17.8K are BC; many items share a label (21 different "Symphony No.9"); list answers need a cap and a deterministic ordering.
  5. Entity-level train/test split — split by QID, not by row, or the same person leaks across both.
  6. Paraphrase model + budget for question variety, plus a verification harness that executes and compares.
  7. Output schema: {"messages": [...]} with QIDs and the source SQL in metadata, never in the training text.

  The time-critical item: notability

  The parser extracts labels, descriptions, and claims — it ignores the sitelinks block. That leaves no way to rank items, and it will visibly wreck the dataset: "who was born in 1473" returns hundreds of people, and the sample I ran earlier surfaced "Jing Li, chemist, born 2000" — a real row, and exactly the kind of nobody that would dominate generated answers.

  Sitelink count is the standard notability proxy, and it buys a second thing: the enwiki title, which is the join key to the Wikipedia markdown dataset if you later want article text for RAG.

  Adding it is ~10–15 lines in parse_item(). The cost of doing it now is the ~40 minutes of run progress, versus a full re-run later or a slow post-hoc API enrichment for hundreds of thousands of items.

  That's the one thing I'd decide today, while the restart is still nearly free. Everything else on the list can wait until the data lands.
